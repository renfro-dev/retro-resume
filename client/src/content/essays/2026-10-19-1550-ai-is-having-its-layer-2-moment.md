_October 19, 2026 · @Rendev_

I think AI is having its Layer 2 moment, and that a lot more of the value is going to land with the people using it.

If you spent time in crypto, this should feel familiar. Ethereum gave us a shared settlement layer, but using it directly could be expensive and slow. Tools like Arbitrum, Optimism, and Base built on that foundation and brought transaction costs down enough to make more applications practical. The underlying technology became easier to actually use. [Ethereum’s overview](https://ethereum.org/layer-2/) explains the mechanics.

I keep coming back to this when I look at what’s happening with AI workspaces.

The models are already capable of doing an enormous amount of useful work. Accessing that capability through chat still leaves you with a job, though if you’re an average user. You gather the context, write the prompt, check the answer, move it into whatever system needs it, and remember to do the whole thing again next week. The ROI narrative for AI isn’t being realized, and we’re in the “this is a bubble” stage of the timeline for that reason. Many enterprises aren’t seeing the value trace back to the books, and SMBs are priced out of enterprise builds.

Ask AI to help with a customer follow-up and you can get a great email. Someone still has to find the last call transcript, check what was promised, look at the account, send the email, and update the CRM. You can spend a surprising amount of your day moving information around for something you bought to save time. Advanced users can evoke skills to do those things, but it’s hard to keep track of, hygiene is a challenge, and adoption is a nightmare.

This is why I’m so bullish on persistent agents. Give the model the right tools, access to the systems where work happens, memory, and a computer that stays on, and you can start handing it an ongoing responsibility. The person can step away without the entire process stopping. That’s a paradigm shift and not enough people are talking about it.

Over seven weeks, we saw [Grok Bot](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot), [Meta’s Muse](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/), and [OpenAI’s Dots](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/whats-new/devday-2026) launch around this idea. The implementations differ, but the direction is pretty clear: agents with computers, access to your tools, and the ability to keep working between conversations, with approvals where needed.

The Layer 2 analogy only goes so far. A model doesn’t give you blockchain security guarantees, and asking a person for approval isn’t settlement. What interests me is the cost of using the underlying capability. In AI, a big part of that cost is human attention. Every step that needs another prompt, another copy and paste, or another reminder eats into the return. Outputs are excessive, so human attention is diminishing. Attention spans are ever smaller.

Consider meeting prep. Before every customer call, an agent checks the CRM, recent emails, Slack conversations, billing history, and the last call transcript. It puts together a brief with the open issues, outstanding commitments, and anything that changed since you last spoke. You review it before the meeting. Very ordinary work. Also very useful. It’s otherwise an enterprise build with a price tag of ~$100K. I can build that today with a $20 per month subscription on multiple platforms. Bullish.

For GTM and RevOps, this is where things get interesting. Account research, CRM hygiene, follow-ups, meeting prep, intent monitoring. There is so much recurring work that sits between systems and depends on someone remembering to move it forward.

Did the follow-up go out? Is the account record accurate? Did the rep walk into the meeting knowing what was happening? Who should I reach out to today?

There’s also an implication for anyone delivering AI as a service. The frontier labs are building the agent workspaces themselves. A lot of the opportunity for the rest of us is in making those agents useful inside a particular business.

That requires understanding how the business works. Which system is authoritative? What qualifies a prospect? Who owns the account? What can the agent change, and what needs approval? What happens when the billing data disagrees with the CRM?

Connecting the tools gets you started. Someone still has to make those decisions.

And giving an agent more autonomy makes the quality of those decisions matter more. If it gets stuck halfway through a workflow, someone needs to know what finished and what didn’t. If it sends the wrong thing to a customer, “the model misunderstood” is a pretty weak explanation. The operator owns the system.

This is where I think a lot of the next wave of ROI will come from: people who understand a business well enough to give agents useful responsibilities, set sensible boundaries, and measure the result. It’s the same process scalability, operational efficiency, and revenue impact that good RevOps work has always been graded on. We have a much more capable set of tools now.

I’m very bullish on that. I’d start with one recurring job, establish what it costs today, and see how much of it an agent can reliably take over. Once the team trusts it, give it the next one.
